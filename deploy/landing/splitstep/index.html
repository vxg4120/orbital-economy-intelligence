The gap in the market

Tennis sensing today makes players choose between price tiers, not product tiers:

SplitStepthe phones already in your bag none

Every incumbent treats sensing as a hardware problem. The observation that SplitStep was founded on is that players already carry the hardware. A modern phone has a millisecond-accurate microphone, a fast camera, and a radio, and when two of them are coordinated, they're a small officiating system. So the missing product was never a device, but rather the orchestration layer.

The inventions

Insight 01 Two phones that only believe what they both heard

Any single microphone can be fooled by wind, by pocket rustle, or by the next court over. The creative leap was to make agreement between devices the filter, so a sound only counts when two phones that are placed apart both witness it in a physically consistent way. That means the noise doesn't get filtered out, because it never gets in at all. This idea, which treats the phones as corroborating witnesses, is the core of the drafted patent application, and it's an area where the prior-art search found open white space.

Insight 02 Sound knows when. Vision knows where. The court knows how big.

Cameras sample time too coarsely to catch a bounce, and microphones catch it to the millisecond but can't see. When you fuse them on one clock, you can measure what neither sensor can measure alone, and the court's own painted lines, which have regulation dimensions, turn four taps on a screen into full metric geometry. There are no tripods, no measuring, and no calibration hardware, because the court calibrates itself.

Insight 03 The second phone is the product, three times over

The same "spare" phone does three jobs. It's the strike microphone that unlocks serve speed and net-fault detection. It's the announcer in your pocket, so the calls are spoken where you stand and not across the court. And it's the remote viewfinder, which puts a live view of the mounted phone in your hand, so you can aim, zoom, and calibrate a camera whose screen you can't see. Competitors ship a companion app for score display, but SplitStep makes the second device a sensor, a speaker, and a control surface.

Insight 04 Follow the courts, not the roadmap

The original wedge was wall practice, until real-world testing surfaced a market fact, which is that practice walls are disappearing from Los Angeles parks. So within a day the product pivoted to serve practice, which can be tested solo because it needs no partner, which is playable on any court, and which is immediately useful. On top of that, every serve that gets recorded is training data that improves the vision models, so it's a feature that funds its own R&D.

Insight 05 Accessories yes, gadgets no

I hold a disciplined line on hardware. SplitStep will recommend a $15 fence clamp, or the Bluetooth earbuds you already own (for voice control from the baseline), but it never manufactures electronics. That keeps the margins software-shaped and avoids inventory and support, and as the freedom-to-operate research showed, it also steers around active patents that cover exactly the net-post camera gadgets that others build.

Strategic judgment

Patent position, built before launch

An adversarial prior-art campaign across commercial, academic, and patent databases mapped the landscape before a single public disclosure. It showed that the multi-device acoustic mechanism sits in identified white space. A provisional application is drafted, and two competitors' patent families were claim-charted to confirm freedom to operate.

A cost structure incumbents can't follow down

Everything runs on the player's own devices, so there's no cloud inference and no per-user compute cost, and it's fully private by construction. The marginal cost of an additional user is effectively zero, and the product also works offline, which is something that cloud-dependent competitors structurally cannot match.

A data flywheel that respects the user

The models launch on licensed and synthetic data, so no user footage is required. But every practice session can, if the player opts in, contribute exactly the real-world imagery and acoustics that the models lack. That way usage compounds into accuracy, and accuracy compounds back into usage.

Execution leverage, managed like a team

I ran an AI engineering pair the way I'd run a high-output team, which meant clear constraints, ruthless priorities, review gates before every release, and a standing rule that nothing ships without a number or a field test. The result was 29 shipped builds, 5 model generations, a patent draft, and a competitive teardown in 11 days, all from one person's direction.

The vision

Serve practice is the wedge. The arc from there is two phones at opposite fences working as a synchronized officiating pair, with line calls that have honest confidence bands, serve speed, match analytics, and a coaching layer on top, which adds up to a consumer Hawk-Eye that's built from hardware the players already own. Feasibility work puts the core capabilities within a 12 to 18 month horizon, and the mechanism that makes it possible is the one that's already drafted for patent.
